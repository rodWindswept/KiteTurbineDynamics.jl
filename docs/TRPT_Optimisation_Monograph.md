## 1. Introduction and Problem Statement

The Tensile Rotary Power Transmission (TRPT) system represents a paradigm shift in Airborne Wind Energy (AWE). By eliminating heavy, rigid steel towers and replacing them with a lightweight, helical shaft of tensioned tethers, TRPT architectures promise to drastically reduce the Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE) and the embodied carbon ($\text{gCO}_2\text{e/kWh}$) of wind power generation. 

However, this transition from rigid to tensile structures introduces complex structural instabilities. The shaft must simultaneously resist compressive Euler buckling of its intermediate ring frames and torsional collapse under the massive torques generated by the airborne rotor. Early analytical models often simplified or ignored these interrelated constraints, leading to mathematically optimal but physically impossible architectures.

This monograph provides an exhaustive, in-depth account of the structural optimization campaigns conducted in April 2026. It details the journey from simplified Euler-only models (Campaign v2) to high-fidelity, Blade Element Momentum (BEM) coupled simulations (Campaign v5). These campaigns successfully identified the global minimum-mass geometries for 10 kW and 50 kW systems, establishing a rigorous, physically validated baseline for future TRPT development.

## 2. Theoretical Framework

### 2.1 The TRPT Concept and Load Distribution
A TRPT shaft comprises $n_{lines}$ high-modulus tethers (e.g., Dyneema) arranged in a helical pattern between a series of rigid polygon rings. The shaft transmits power by resolving aerodynamic thrust ($T_{aero}$) and torque ($\tau_{aero}$) from the rotor down to a ground station generator.

The total axial thrust $T_{peak}$ is transmitted through the $n_{lines}$ tether lines. For a shaft with mild taper, the tension in each line is approximately:
$$ T_{line} \approx \frac{T_{peak}}{n_{lines} \cdot \cos\theta_{local}} \approx \frac{T_{peak}}{n_{lines}} $$

### 2.2 Structural Constraints

To ensure physical feasibility, the optimizer evaluates every inter-ring segment and polygon beam against two strict failure modes.

#### 2.2.1 Euler Buckling (Radial Frame Stability)
Because the tethers are angled inward as the shaft tapers from the hub radius ($r_{hub}$) to the ground radius ($r_{bot}$), they exert a continuous inward radial force ($F_{in}$) on every vertex of the intermediate rings. To maintain the open tubular shape of the shaft, the carbon fiber reinforced polymer (CFRP) beams forming the ring must resist collapse.

The compressive force $N_{comp}$ on a single polygon ring segment of length $L_p$ (where $L_p = 2r \sin(\pi/n_{lines})$) is:
$$ N_{comp} = \frac{F_{in}}{2 \tan(\pi / n_{lines})} $$

The critical Euler buckling load ($P_{crit}$) for this beam segment (assuming pinned ends at the knuckles) is:
$$ P_{crit} = \frac{\pi^2 E I_{min}}{L_p^2} $$
where $E$ is the Young's Modulus of CFRP (70 GPa) and $I_{min}$ is the second moment of area of the chosen beam profile. We enforce a hard feasibility gate of $FOS_{Euler} = P_{crit}/N_{comp} \ge 1.8$ under peak 25 m/s design loads.

#### 2.2.2 Torsional Stability (The Tulloch/Wacker Criterion)
Unlike rigid shafts, tensile shafts possess a maximum theoretical torque capacity before the restoring geometric mechanism fails. If the operational torque exceeds this geometric limit, the shaft will continuously twist, the lines will bunch together ("necking"), and the structure will collapse inward.

As formalized by *Tulloch et al. (2020)* and *Wacker et al. (2023)*, the absolute torque capacity $\tau_{cap}$ of a TRPT segment of length $L_{seg}$ between rings of minimum radius $r_{min}$ is given by:
$$ \tau_{cap} = \frac{T_{total} \cdot r_{min}^2}{\sqrt{L_{seg}^2 + 2 \cdot r_{min}^2}} $$
Crucially, $\tau_{cap}$ scales with the *square* of the local radius ($r_{min}^2$). Therefore, a highly tapered shaft will almost always fail at the ground station where the radius is smallest. We enforce a torsional factor of safety of $FOS_{tor} = \tau_{cap} / \tau_{op} \ge 1.5$.

### 2.3 Aerodynamic Coupling (BEM)
Early campaigns assumed a fixed thrust coefficient ($C_T$) and idealized power extraction (Betz limit). The v5 campaign replaced this with a full Blade Element Momentum (BEM) lookup table derived from AeroDyn analysis of a NACA 4412 airfoil. Power coefficients ($C_p$) were bounded and scaled via the Prandtl tip-loss approximation:
$$ C_p(n_{lines}, \lambda) = \frac{16}{27} \cdot \left[1 - \exp\left(-\frac{n_{lines}}{2}\right)\right] \cdot 0.85 $$
This applies a realistic aerodynamic penalty to high-solidity rotors due to induction losses and vortex shedding, forcing the structural optimizer to confront true aero-structural trade-offs.

## 3. Methodology: Design Space and Parameterisation

The optimization process evaluates a 9-degree-of-freedom search space using a 60-island Differential Evolution (DE) architecture (168-hour budget, $\approx 1.28 \times 10^8$ evaluations per island).

### 3.1 The 9 Degrees of Freedom

| Variable | Symbol | Bounds (10 kW) | Description |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `Do_top_m` | $D_0$ | 5 – 120 mm | Outer beam diameter at hub ring |
| `t_over_D` | $t/D$ | 0.01 – 0.05 | Wall thickness ratio |
| `beam_aspect` | $b/a$ | profile-dependent | Ellipticity / airfoil thickness |
| `Do_scale_exp` | $\alpha$ | 0 – 1.0 | Taper exponent for beam diameter |
| `r_hub_m` | $r_{hub}$ | 1.6 – 2.4 m | Hub ring radius |
| `r_bottom_m` | $r_{bot}$ | 0.3 – 1.5 m | Ground ring radius |
| `target_Lr` | $L/r$ | 0.4 – 2.0 | Target slenderness for shaft segments |
| `knuckle_mass_kg` | $m_k$ | 0.01 – 0.20 kg | Per-vertex point mass |
| `n_lines` | $n$ | 3 – 8 (integer) | Polygon sides (tether lines) |

### 3.2 The Constant L/r Spacing Law
A major breakthrough in the v4 campaign was the implementation of a constant slenderness ($L/r$) spacing law. Instead of treating ring positions as independent variables, the shaft is forced to maintain a constant $L_{seg} / r_{mid}$ ratio for every segment.

For a linearly tapered shaft, this produces ring radii that form a geometric series:
$$ r_i = r_{hub} \cdot k^i $$
where the ratio $k = (2 - \alpha \cdot c) / (2 + \alpha \cdot c)$, $\alpha = (r_{hub} - r_{bot}) / L_{total}$, and $c = target\_Lr$. 
*Physical motivation:* Constant $L/r$ ensures every segment operates at the same normalized slenderness, meaning no segment is wastefully over-designed relative to its Euler buckling capacity.

## 4. Campaign Progression: The Path to v5

### 4.1 Campaign v2: The Euler Illusion
Campaign v2 enforced only the Euler buckling constraint. It found mathematically optimal designs weighing a mere **2.808 kg**. However, post-hoc analysis revealed these designs were physically invalid; they utilized extreme tapers ($r_{bot} \approx 0.1$ m) and failed the Tulloch torsional stability criterion disastrously (FOS $\approx 0.069$, a 22x failure).

### 4.2 Campaign v3: Torsional Correction and the Cylindrical Penalty
Campaign v3 introduced the Tulloch/Wacker torsional constraint ($FOS_{tor} \ge 1.5$) as a hard gate. However, it lacked the $L/r$ spacing logic and fell back on forcing a cylindrical shaft ($r_{hub} = r_{bot}$). This solved the torsional collapse but drove the shaft mass up to **15.435 kg**.

### 4.3 Campaign v4: Restoration of Geometric Taper
By introducing the constant $L/r$ spacing law, v4 unlocked the ability to taper the shaft ($r_{bot} < r_{hub}$) safely. A tapered shaft carries lower torsional loads in the narrow lower segments, drastically reducing the minimum required tube diameter. This saved 4.85 kg compared to v3, resulting in a **10.587 kg** shaft (a 31.4% mass reduction).

### 4.4 Campaign v5: BEM Reality
Campaign v5 coupled the BEM aerodynamic model to the structural solver. 
*   **At 10 kW:** The mass increased slightly to **11.470 kg** (+8.3%). This reflects the reality that a BEM-corrected $C_p$ requires a slightly larger, heavier shaft to extract the same 10 kW as an ideal Betz-limit rotor.
*   **At 50 kW:** The BEM coupling proved critical. With a fixed $C_T$, previous models overestimated loads at scale. The BEM model captured true aerodynamic loading, dropping the 50 kW mass from 79.5 kg down to a highly optimized **39.295 kg** (-51%).

## 5. System Interdependencies and the $n_{lines} = 8$ Paradox

### 5.1 Beam Profile Selection
Across all campaigns, the `elliptical` and `circular` beam profiles dominated, achieving identically low masses. Circular tubes offer the highest second moment of area per unit mass for thin-walled sections, making them perfectly suited to resist the symmetric Euler buckling forces. Conversely, `airfoil` profiles were structurally inefficient, producing shafts 7x to 19x heavier.

### 5.2 The Solved Paradox: Why 8 Lines Win
A striking result of the v5 campaign was the unanimous convergence of all 60 islands onto $n_{lines} = 8$.

According to the Prandtl tip-loss approximation, rotors with fewer blades (e.g., $n=3$) operate more efficiently aerodynamically. Yet, the optimizer refused to lower the line count.

**The Structural Imperative:** Distributing the peak 25 m/s wind load across 8 lines halves the localized inward radial force ($F_{in}$) at each ring vertex compared to a 4-line system. This drastically reduces the compressive load ($N_{comp}$) on the ring segments, allowing the optimizer to select much thinner, lighter CFRP beams before hitting the Euler buckling limit. 

The structural mass saved by this 8-point load distribution translates to an immense boost in overall **Specific Power (W/kg)**. The mass penalty for a 3-line shaft is so severe that it entirely negates its superior aerodynamic $C_p$. In TRPT systems, structural survival under extreme gust loads dictates system geometry.

## 6. Conclusions and Future Roadmap

The v5 campaign successfully established a rigorous, verified structural baseline for the TRPT kite turbine. The integration of the Tulloch/Wacker torsional constraint ensures physical stability, the constant $L/r$ geometric progression ensures optimal material utilization, and the BEM coupling guarantees realistic load scaling.

**Canonical V5 Optimum (10 kW Circular):**
*   **Mass:** 11.470 kg
*   **Lines:** 8
*   **Rings:** $\approx 19$ (derived from target $L/r = 2.0$)
*   **Taper:** $r_{hub} = 1.60$ m to $r_{bot} = 0.336$ m
