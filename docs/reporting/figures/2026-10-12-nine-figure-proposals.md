## Figure 1 — System anatomy and terminology diagram

The first recommended figure is a labelled system-anatomy diagram that establishes the physical vocabulary before the reader encounters the genome, evaluator, or results. The layout should be a clean isometric or slightly oblique side view of the full kite-turbine machine, with the airborne TRPT structure at the centre, the lifter stack above or to one side, and the ground station below or outside the main frame as a dashed boundary. The central machine should show six rings, three tension lines between rings, the active rotor blades on the hub ring, spacer rings without blades, the lift line, the sky anchor, the cyan line, the lift bearing, the bridle set, and the path down to the ground generator or PTO station. Each element should be drawn with a distinct but restrained colour: structural rings in neutral grey or blue-grey, TRPT lines in dark cyan or black, blades in a warm accent such as amber, and the lift chain in a second accent such as teal. The visual hierarchy should make the load path obvious: the reader’s eye should move from lifter to sky anchor, down the lift line, through the bearing and bridle, into the rotor, then along the TRPT rings toward the ground station.

The text on the figure should be minimal but precise. A title such as “Kite turbine anatomy: ground-generation rotary AWES with TRPT” is appropriate. Labels should use short noun phrases rather than full sentences: “lifter stack,” “lift line,” “sky anchor,” “lift bearing,” “bridle,” “main rotor,” “TRPT ring R1–R6,” “tension line,” “ground ring,” and “ground generator / PTO station.” A small inset or side panel can define the three most confusing terms: “ring,” “line,” and “rotor.” The data type is primarily categorical and symbolic, with optional dimensional callouts for the winner geometry: hub radius 4.580 m, ground radius 0.804 m, six rings, three lines, one active rotor. If dimensions are included, they should be secondary to topology so that the figure remains useful even if the winner changes in a later campaign. The relation to the text is direct: this figure supports the Introduction’s explanation of ground-gen versus fly-gen machines and Methods §2.1’s definition of rings, lines, rotors, spacers, bank angle, and blade scale. It should also prepare the reader for the genome decode strip by showing which physical features correspond to design genes.

The suggested format is a vector-first SVG or PDF diagram with a high-resolution PNG fallback for web or presentation use. If the report is rendered through Pandoc, the figure should be embedded as an external SVG where possible, because labels and thin line work will remain crisp in print. A raster fallback should be at least 300 dpi at final page width. The colour palette should be colour-blind safe, using shape and label position rather than hue alone to distinguish components. Line weights should vary by function: thick for primary load path, medium for structural rings, thin for bridle or secondary lines. A scale bar of 5 m is useful if the figure includes physical proportions. Suggested ranges are not numeric axes but visual bounds: keep labels within the page margins, avoid leader-line crossings, and limit callouts to no more than twelve primary labels. If a label must be long, split it into a two-line caption rather than shrinking the type.

The subtext or caption should explain that the diagram is schematic and not to scale unless a scale bar is present. A good caption might read: “Schematic anatomy of the ground-generation rotary kite turbine. The lifter provides lift only; torque is transmitted through the tensioned TRPT rings to the ground station.” This sentence prevents a common misreading: the reader might otherwise assume the lifter also drives the rotor. A second line can state that ring numbering runs from ground to hub or from hub to ground, depending on the convention used in the register, because the report currently references R1 through R6 and S1 through S5. Comments for the figure maker: do not turn this into a full engineering drawing with every bolt, bearing, or tether attachment. The purpose is orientation. If the lifter stack is a stack of autogyros, show it as a simplified multi-rotor silhouette rather than detailed blades. If the PTO station is outside the render scope, show it as a dashed ground block and label it “outside primary render scope” if needed. The figure should make the reader feel that they can name every major component before reaching the results.

Figure 2 — Genome-to-machine decode strip
The second recommended figure is a genome-to-machine decode strip that explains how the search represents a candidate design and how that representation becomes a buildable machine. The layout should be horizontal, divided into three connected zones: left, the genome vector; centre, the decoder rules; right, the built machine summary. The genome zone should show the design vector as a compact sequence of fields or chips, each labelled with a gene name such as “ring geometry,” “polygon count,” “rotor count,” “bank angle,” and “blade scale.” Each chip can display the winner value where known: three lines, six rings, one active rotor, bank 7.088° at ring 6, blade scale 0.82 on the active rotor, and 0.20 in an inactive slot. The centre zone should show arrows from each gene to its physical effect: polygon count maps to line attachments per ring; rotor count maps to active versus spacer rings; bank angle maps to blade orientation about the chord axis at the ring; blade scale maps to blade size; ring geometry maps to radii and spacing. The right zone should contain a small simplified machine icon or miniature render with corresponding features highlighted, so the reader sees that the genome is not an abstract vector but a direct recipe for a physical configuration.

The text on the figure should be explanatory but compact. A title such as “Genome decode: from design vector to built geometry” works well. Each gene chip should carry its unit or type: integer for counts, degrees for bank angle, dimensionless factor for blade scale, metres or relative radii for ring geometry if available. The decoder zone should include one or two short rule statements, for example “rotor count selects active rings” and “inactive slots remain in the vector but instantiate no blades.” This last statement is important because the report mentions a blade scale of 0.20 on a rotor that does not exist; without this visual explanation, that phrase may confuse readers. The data types are mixed: discrete integers for counts, continuous values for angles and scales, and categorical flags for active/inactive rotor status. The figure should make clear which genes were actually decoded in the winner and which were present but inactive. The relation to the text is central: this figure supports Methods §2.2 and Results §3.3, where the winner genome is described. It also helps the reader understand why the search can propose invalid or unbuildable genomes, because the decoder sits between the optimizer and the builder.

The suggested format is a vector diagram with a small table-like legend, again SVG-first with PNG fallback. The figure should use rounded rectangles for gene chips, arrows with clear directionality, and a consistent iconography for active versus inactive elements. Active rotor genes can be filled; inactive genes can be outlined or greyed out. If the genome contains more fields than are shown in the winner, the figure can include an ellipsis or a “remaining fields” chip to avoid overcrowding. Suggested ranges should be shown only if the register defines them: for example, polygon count range, bank angle range, blade scale range, and ring count range. If exact bounds are not yet signed, the figure should avoid inventing them and instead label the visible values as “winner value” rather than implying a full search range. A useful subtext line could be: “The genome is the design vector; the decoder translates it into buildable geometry; the builder constructs the machine for evaluation.” Another line can state: “Inactive gene slots are retained for decoder compatibility but do not create physical blades in this campaign.”

Comments for the figure maker: this figure should not try to show every possible mutation or island history. Its job is to demystify the search representation. If the report later adds a full landscape figure, this strip can remain as the conceptual bridge between optimization and geometry. The visual style should match the system anatomy diagram so that the reader recognizes the same machine features. A small inset showing an invalid genome being rejected by the decoder could be useful if space allows, because it connects to the failure ledger’s lesson that the decoder must refuse what the builder cannot make. However, if the figure becomes too busy, move that idea to the failure-ledger timeline instead. The final takeaway should be simple: a candidate is not just a number; it is a structured description of rings, lines, rotors, blades, and scales, and the winner’s numbers correspond to specific physical choices.

Figure 3 — Measurement-window evaluation timeline and gate map
The third recommended figure is a measurement-window evaluation timeline and gate map that explains what the evaluator measures, when it measures it, and why certain transients are excluded. The layout should be a horizontal time axis from 0 to approximately 50 seconds, divided into two main phases: a relax phase of about 10 seconds and a measurement window of about 40 seconds. Above or below the timeline, separate swimlanes can show state variables, gate activity, and path-specific behaviour. One lane can show angular speed or power over time, with the startup transient visible but visually de-emphasised during the relax phase. Another lane can show gate status as coloured blocks: rope-break detection, power floor, FoS, clearance, twist, and NaN/Inf guards. A third lane can distinguish the ODE/cold path from the rapid/warm path, showing where the warm path’s injected velocities create a transient that trips the rope-break gate even though the machine would pass under the corrected ruling. The visual emphasis should make clear that the reported performance numbers come from the measurement window, not from the initial settle behaviour.

The text on the figure should define “measurement window” directly in the caption or as an in-figure label. Suggested labels include “relax: 10 s,” “measurement window: 40 s,” “startup transient excluded,” “gate active during window,” and “rope-break gate: window only under ruling.” The data types are time series, boolean gate states, and categorical path labels. Numeric traces can include speed in rad/s and power in kW; the winner’s settle state starts around 10.170 rad/s, and the mean power over the window is 5.309 kW, so a plausible vertical range for speed might be 8 to 12 rad/s and for power 4 to 7 kW. If exact trace data are available, use them; if not, the figure can show idealised traces with labelled values rather than fake precision. The relation to the text is strong: this figure supports Methods §2.3, where the evaluator, load law, relax time, window, and deterministic stepping are described, and Discussion §4.1, where the rapid path rejects winners because of gate placement during the relax run. It also clarifies the statement that the analysis pipeline is treated with distrust, because the figure shows exactly which parts of the simulation are trusted for scoring.

The suggested format is a vector timeline chart, preferably SVG or PDF, with optional interactive HTML version if the report is published online. The style should resemble an engineering timing diagram: clean horizontal bands, sharp phase boundaries, and minimal decorative shading. Use a muted background for the relax phase and a brighter or bordered band for the measurement window so that the scoring interval is unmistakable. Gate lanes can use filled rectangles for active gates and hatched or grey rectangles for inactive or not-yet-applicable gates. If the rope-break gate is shown as disabled during relax under the desired ruling, use a clear visual cue such as an open outline or a lock icon labelled “off during relax.” Suggested ranges: time axis 0 to 50 s; speed axis 8 to 12 rad/s if using the winner; power axis 4.5 to 6.5 kW if showing power. If multiple designs are shown, limit the figure to three representative cases: a healthy cold-path winner, a warm-path false reject, and perhaps a genuine failure. A subtext line could read: “Performance is scored only over the measurement window; relax transients are used to settle the state but not to judge structural breaks.”

Comments for the figure maker: this figure is one of the most important for credibility because it separates machine behaviour from evaluator artefacts. It should avoid implying that the warm path is always wrong; instead, it should show that the current default path has a known gate-placement issue. If the report wants to preserve internal terminology, include a small legend mapping “ODE path” to :cold and “rapid path” to :warm, but do not make code names the primary labels. The figure should also make clear that determinism is about reproducibility under fixed seeds and configuration, not about eliminating all transients. A useful interpretive comment in the caption is: “The measurement window is the instrument’s measurement aperture; it exists so that startup artefacts do not masquerade as operating performance.” This framing helps the reader understand why a design can look unstable early but still be valid, and why a rapid path can reject a healthy machine if it measures the wrong interval.

Figure 4 — Campaign architecture, island budget, and status mix
The fourth recommended figure is a campaign architecture and status-mix diagram that explains how the 930-design search was organised and what fraction of candidates survived the gates. The layout should be split into two connected parts. The upper part can show the differential-evolution campaign structure: three islands running in parallel, each with its own population and drift, each evaluating 310 designs, all starting from a common seed or shared configuration where appropriate. Use three rounded rectangles or island nodes labelled “Island 1,” “Island 2,” and “Island 3,” with arrows showing generation breeding, culling, and evaluation budget. A small central node can represent the common campaign configuration: fixed k, honest window, FoS gate, line length, and random seed policy. The lower part should show the outcome as a funnel or stacked bar chart: total evaluated designs, then status categories ok, reject, clearance_reject, and reject_twist. The counts from the report are 930 total, 508 ok, 301 reject, 110 clearance_reject, and 11 reject_twist. These numbers should be visible directly on the bars or funnel segments.

The text on the figure should define each status in plain language. A title such as “Campaign architecture and gate outcomes” is appropriate. Labels can read: “3 islands,” “310 evaluations per island,” “930 total evaluations,” “ok: passed all gates,” “reject: failed primary gate,” “clearance_reject: failed clearance gate,” and “reject_twist: failed twist gate.” If the exact gate names are still pending Track B or Track D approval, use provisional labels and add a caption note that final gate terminology will be ratified. The data types are counts, categorical statuses, and campaign metadata. The figure should make clear that the search is not a single monolithic population but three semi-independent populations with their own drift, which helps explain why island 3 can produce a different winner than islands 1 and 2. It also supports the report’s claim that the campaign is reproducible under fixed seeds and configuration, because the upper architecture panel can include a small provenance chip: “fixed seed per island,” “commit pinned,” “configuration pinned.” The relation to the text is direct: this figure belongs with Methods §2.4 and Results §3.1, where the campaign scale and status mix are introduced.

The suggested format is a vector Sankey-like funnel or a combined architecture-plus-bar chart in SVG/PDF. If using a Sankey diagram, the flow should move from “930 evaluated” into four status streams, with widths proportional to counts. If the visual style needs to remain simple, use a horizontal stacked bar for the status mix beneath the island diagram. The colour palette should distinguish pass from fail without relying on red/green alone: for example, blue for ok, amber for reject, grey for clearance_reject, and purple for reject_twist. Suggested ranges are straightforward: total count 930; category counts as above; percentage labels can be added if useful, such as 54.6% ok, 32.4% reject, 11.8% clearance reject, and 1.2% twist reject. A subtext line could read: “The campaign is a screen, not an exhaustive survey; status counts show how the gates shape the feasible region.” Another useful line is: “All three islands use the same evaluation window and gate definitions unless stated otherwise.”

Comments for the figure maker: avoid making the island diagram look like a corporate org chart. It should read as an optimization process. If the report later includes convergence plots, this figure can remain the high-level map, while the convergence figure shows temporal progress. The status mix is valuable because it quantifies how restrictive the gates are. A reader who sees 508 ok designs out of 930 may infer that the feasible region is reasonably broad, but the convergence and winner figures will then show that mass or power still varied among survivors. If internal notes say “gate names await Track B,” do not print that in the main figure; instead, use a caption footnote such as “Status definitions are provisional pending final gate glossary.” The interpretive point is that the campaign does not merely generate designs; it filters them through physical and operational constraints, and the resulting status mix is evidence that the search space contains both promising candidates and structurally or geometrically invalid ones.

Figure 5 — Island convergence history and feasible power band
The fifth recommended figure is an island convergence history chart that shows how each island’s best candidate improved over generations and where the winner emerged. The layout should be a multi-panel line or scatter plot with generation number on the x-axis, from 0 to at least 30, and the campaign objective or best mass metric on the y-axis. If the objective is mass minimization, label the axis clearly as “best island score (kg)” or “best objective value (kg),” and add a caption note if that metric differs from final airborne mass. The three islands should be separate lines or point series: Island 1 reaching 30.578 kg at generation 30, Island 2 reaching 30.637 kg at generation 30, and Island 3 reaching 27.364 kg at generation 26. The seed value of 36.109 kg can be shown as a horizontal reference line or an initial point at generation 0. A secondary panel or overlaid band can show the surviving ok designs’ mean power range, reported as 5.02 to 6.32 kW, so the reader sees that convergence in mass did not come from abandoning the power requirement. The winner’s final airborne mass of 25.698 kg can be marked separately if it is not identical to the island best score, with a note explaining the difference in metric or era.

The text on the figure should be concise but numerically explicit. A title such as “Convergence by island and generation” works well. Labels should include “Island 1,” “Island 2,” “Island 3,” “seed score,” “island best,” and “winner.” If the y-axis is mass, units must be visible; if power is shown, use kW. The data types are numeric time-series values: generation count, objective value, and optionally mean power or FoS. The relation to the text is central: this figure belongs with Results §3.2, where island convergence is reported, and it also supports the later statement that island 3 carries the winner. It helps the reader understand that the winner did not appear by chance at the final generation but emerged from an island whose trajectory diverged from the other two. If the report’s “kg” values are campaign scores rather than directly comparable physical masses, this figure is the place to prevent misinterpretation. A caption line could read: “The convergence metric is the campaign objective as recorded in the register; final physical airborne mass is reported separately for the winner.”

The suggested format is a vector line plot with point markers, SVG-first and PNG fallback. Use distinct line styles as well as colours so the figure remains readable in print and for colour-blind readers: for example, solid blue for Island 1, dashed amber for Island 2, and dotted purple for Island 3. Add subtle gridlines but avoid heavy chart junk. If the convergence is noisy, plot the best value per generation rather than every evaluation, unless a separate scatter of all ok designs is useful. Suggested ranges: x-axis 0 to 30 generations; y-axis approximately 26 to 37 kg if showing seed and island bests. If power is shown as a secondary band, use a right-hand axis or a lower panel with range 4.5 to 6.5 kW. A subtext line could state: “Island 3 reaches the lowest recorded score at generation 26; islands 1 and 2 continue to generation 30 but remain higher.” Another line can note that the search stopped early as a screen, not a survey, so convergence should not be read as proof of global optimality.

Comments for the figure maker: this figure should not overstate the meaning of the y-axis. If there is any ambiguity between “score,” “mass,” and “airborne mass,” resolve it in the caption before publication. The reader needs to know whether a lower value is always better. If the objective includes penalties or constraints, say so. If the winner’s 25.698 kg appears below the island best of 27.364 kg, explain that this may reflect a difference between campaign score and final decoded physical mass, or between pre-fold and post-fold representations. Without that explanation, the figure may look internally inconsistent. The interpretive value is that the search produced a meaningful improvement over the seed and identified a promising region, while still leaving room for further exploration. A good visual cue is to highlight the winner point with a star or ring marker, but do not let the highlight dominate the island comparison.

Figure 6 — Winner anatomy with lift chain and operating point
The sixth recommended figure is a labelled winner-anatomy render that combines geometry, lift path, and key operating numbers into one interpretable image. The layout should be an annotated side elevation or isometric view of the island-3 winner machine, with the six rings clearly numbered from ground to hub or according to the report’s convention. The active rotor should be visually distinct, with blades shown at their bank angle and blade scale. The lift chain should be highlighted as a continuous path: lifter stack above, lift line descending to the sky anchor or three-way knot, then down the cyan line to the lift bearing, then through bridles to the main rotor. A dashed outline can indicate the ground station or PTO station if it is outside the primary render scope. Dimension callouts should show hub radius 4.580 m and ground radius 0.804 m. A small inset panel can show the lift-line geometry: elevation angle 70°, vertical load 304.6 N, line tension 324.1 N, sizing mass 20.70 kg, full airborne mass 25.70 kg, and vertical lift margin 1.21. Another inset or side table can list operating values: mean power 5.309 kW, end power 5.314 kW, minimum FoS 14.972, airborne mass 25.698 kg, settle speed 10.170 rad/s, and window swing 0.029 kW.

The text on the figure should be carefully limited to labels that help the reader verify the machine against the register. A title such as “Island-3 winner: geometry, lift chain, and operating point” is appropriate. Labels should include “lifter stack,” “lift line,” “sky anchor,” “cyan line,” “lift bearing,” “bridle,” “R1 ground ring,” “R6 hub ring,” “active rotor,” and “TRPT lines.” The data types are mixed: geometric dimensions, categorical component labels, scalar operating metrics, and force calculations. The figure should make clear that the lifter provides lift only and does not provide torque or drive; this is a critical conceptual point in the report. A small equation inset can show the lift-margin definition, but it must be unambiguous. If the margin of 1.21 is based on vertical load divided by total airborne weight, state that explicitly: “vertical lift margin = vertical component of lift force / total airborne weight.” If instead a different definition is intended, correct the formula before publication. The relation to the text is direct: this figure belongs with Results §3.3, where the winner’s geometry, lifter sizing, mass, power, and FoS are described. It also supports the conclusion that the winner survives the full window under the modeled assumptions.

The suggested format is a vector render with callouts, SVG/PDF first and PNG fallback. The machine should be drawn from the built geometry rather than a generic icon, because the report already has a machine renderer and the figure claims geometry only. Use a neutral background so that structural margins can be added later if needed. A scale bar of 5 m is useful. Suggested ranges for callouts: radii in metres to three decimals as registered; angles in degrees; forces in newtons; power in kW; mass in kg; FoS dimensionless. If the figure includes the lift-line inset, use a small right-triangle diagram showing the 70° elevation angle and the vertical/horizontal force components. A subtext line could read: “The lift chain is sized for the required vertical load; the lifter modulation assumption is stated separately in the main text.” Another line can state: “Operating values are from the honest window unless labelled as settle-state values.”

Comments for the figure maker: this figure should not become a dashboard of every possible metric. Choose the few numbers that define the winner and trust the register for the rest. The most important visual task is to make the load path legible: lift comes from above, structural torque transmission runs through the TRPT rings, and the ground station receives power. If the active rotor’s blade scale is 0.82 and an inactive slot has 0.20, do not draw a second physical rotor unless it actually exists; instead, show the inactive slot only in the genome figure. The bank angle of 7.088° at ring 6 can be shown as a small angular callout if it is visually meaningful, but if it is too small to see, place it in the side table rather than forcing a tiny diagram. The interpretive point is that the winner is not just a performance number; it is a coherent physical machine with a plausible lift path and registered structural margins.

Figure 7 — Structural and twist margin dashboard
The seventh recommended figure is a structural and twist margin dashboard that shows how far the winner operates below failure boundaries in both ring beam-column interaction and segment twist/torque capacity. The layout should be a multi-panel figure with three or four related panels. Panel A can show ring utilization as stacked horizontal bars for each checked ring, R2 through R6, with axial share and bending share stacked to form the total beam-column interaction. The fail boundary at unit utilization should be drawn as a dashed vertical line at 1.0. Panel B can show ring factor of safety as either a separate bar chart or readout labels at the end of each bar; because one FoS value is extremely large, use a logarithmic axis or cap the visual bar with an explicit text label such as “FoS = 326,974” to avoid compressing all other values. Panel C can show transmitted twist for shaft segments S1 through S5 against their over-twist limits δcrit, using paired bars or a line-with-limit plot. Panel D can pair segment torque with torsional capacity at the same capture instant, again using paired bars or dot plots. The visual logic should be: each panel compares demand to capacity, and the reader should immediately see that all checked elements are below their relevant limits.

The text on the figure should define utilization, FoS, twist angle, and torque capacity without assuming prior knowledge. A title such as “Winner margins: ring utilization, twist, and torsional capacity” is suitable. Labels should include “axial share,” “bending share,” “fail boundary = 1.0,” “ring FoS = 1 / utilization,” “transmitted twist |Δα|,” “δcrit over-twist limit,” “segment torque,” and “torsional capacity.” The data types are dimensionless utilization, dimensionless FoS, angles in degrees, and torque in N·m. Suggested ranges: utilization axis 0 to 1.05; FoS axis logarithmic from 1 to 1e6 or capped at 1e5 with true values labelled; twist axis 0 to 100° if the observed values are around 20–100°; torque axis 0 to 1100 N·m based on the reported paired values. The figure should note that the ground ring R1 is not checked because it is ground-supported, and that torsion may be tracked outside the beam-column interaction formula. If some segments have “no limit” because their tether geometry does not create an over-twist ceiling, show them as hatched or open markers with a label explaining that tether break strain and ring compression carry the limit instead.

The suggested format is a vector dashboard in SVG/PDF with PNG fallback. Use consistent colours across panels: demand bars in one hue, capacity or fail boundary in another, and “no limit” cases in a neutral hatch. Avoid using red for all high values; reserve red only for values near or above the limit, which in this winner are none. A subtext line could read: “All margins are evaluated at the final integration step of the honest window capture.” Another line can state: “Capacity and load are paired at the same simulation state; do not compare a torque value to a capacity from a different instant.” This is important because the report explicitly warns against mismatched pairing. The relation to the text is direct: this figure belongs with Results §3.4 and replaces or improves the existing ring-utilisation and twist-limit figures by placing them in one coherent margin story. It also supports the discussion of model boundaries, because it shows which structural checks are present and which are not, such as blade integrity.

Comments for the figure maker: this figure should be numerically conservative. If a FoS value in the figure differs slightly from the campaign minimum FoS in the register, explain whether the difference is due to capture instant, ring-specific check, or row definition. Do not let the extreme R5-like FoS value dominate the visual scale; use log scaling or a broken axis with clear annotation. The interpretive message is not merely “the machine is safe,” but “the checked TRPT elements have large margins at this operating point.” That distinction matters because blade integrity is not gated in the current model. A useful caption sentence is: “The dashboard covers the modeled structural checks; it does not include blade root failure, fatigue, or unmodelled bridling drag.” This keeps the figure honest and connects it to the limitations section.

Figure 8 — Machine evolution strip from seed to winner

The layout should be a horizontal strip of seven panels, each containing one machine render at the same physical scale. The panels can correspond to generations 0, 6, 10, 18, 22, 25, and 26, as in the report. Each panel should show the built state of that genome: ring segments, TRPT lines between rings, blade segments, and the swept annulus of the main rotor if useful. A subtle arrow or gradient background can indicate progression from left to right, but the primary evidence should be the changing geometry, not decorative motion cues. The first panel should be labelled “seed” or “generation 0,” and the final panel should be labelled “winner” or “generation 26.” If the report wants to preserve the claim that the strip shows geometry only, avoid printing power or mass values inside the panels; instead, place any performance context in the caption or a separate convergence figure. A scale bar of 5 m should appear once in the footer or repeated faintly in each panel if needed for readability.

The text on the figure should be minimal but sufficient to identify what changed. A title such as “Island-3 geometry evolution from seed to winner” is appropriate. Each panel can carry a small generation label and, if helpful, a short geometric tag such as “6 rings,” “3 lines,” or “1 active rotor.” The data types are geometric: ring count, radii, line attachments, blade presence, blade scale, and overall proportions. The figure should make visible the search’s physical trajectory: whether early candidates were larger, more complex, more symmetric, or structurally different from the final winner. If the winner has a hub radius of 4.580 m and ground radius of 0.804 m, those values can appear in the final panel or in a caption table, but they should not clutter every intermediate panel. The relation to the text is direct: this figure belongs with Results §3.5 and supports the narrative that the search moved through a design space rather than jumping directly to an isolated optimum. It also helps explain why the report treats the campaign as a screen: the strip shows a path, but not the full landscape.

The suggested format is a vector render strip in SVG/PDF with PNG fallback. All seven panels must share one scale and one projection angle so that size differences are meaningful. If the current renderer uses 0.0602 m per pixel on a 1580 by 596 px canvas, that can be preserved for consistency, but the final figure should be redrawn at print resolution rather than relying on screen pixels. Use consistent section colours for ring segments and TRPT lines across all panels. A scale bar of 5 m is strongly recommended. Suggested ranges are physical, not axis-based: show only the actual built sizes from the register; do not normalise each machine to fit its panel independently. A subtext line could read: “The strip claims geometry only; it does not display power or mass values.” Another line can state: “All panels share one scale, so apparent size differences reflect built-size differences.”

Comments for the figure maker: this figure is visually powerful but easy to make misleading if scales are inconsistent. The most important rule is that the same physical length must occupy the same number of points in every panel. If a machine appears smaller because it is truly smaller, that is evidence; if it appears smaller because it was fitted into the frame, that is not. Avoid adding arrows between panels unless they help show a specific change, such as reduction in ring count or growth in blade scale. The interpretive point is that the winner is the endpoint of a search path, not a standalone invention. If Phase 3 analysis later identifies which genes changed most, this strip can be enhanced with small gene-change annotations above each transition, but the base figure should remain clean. A good caption sentence is: “The evolution strip shows how island 3’s best built geometry changed over selected generations, ending at the registered winner.”

Figure 9 — Failure ledger and model-era timeline
The ninth recommended figure is a failure-ledger and model-era timeline that visualises the report’s error history, voided data, near-misses, and lessons learned. The layout should be a horizontal timeline spanning the relevant development period, for example August to October 2026, with major events placed as cards or milestone markers along the axis.
